Variable Star Discovery

On the 7th of January 2022 I was imaging NGC 1333 in the UK with my William Optics Z73 telescope and ASI294MC Pro camera. I captured a total of 14 hours of unfiltered broadband data over 3 nights, made a nice image and didn’t think much more about it. A couple of years later Dylan O’Donnell released a YouTube video about an asteroid he had found while blinking his dataset. I thought that was pretty cool and gave it a try myself on all my archived image datasets but couldn’t find anything.

In 2025 I noticed a PixInsight script from SetiAstro called Supernova Asteroid Hunter. I thought, “Cool, this will automatically highlight any asteroids that I didn’t spot by blinking my image datasets!” The script detects differences between a base image and other images in your data by basically overlaying one on top of the other. If the difference in pixel value is larger than an adjustable threshold, the script will highlight a region of interest for further inspection. The caveat here is that it struggles with false detections caused by hot and cold pixels, and cosmic rays. To check a detection, I simply used Blink in PixInsight and matched the area in the Blink animation to the position of the detection.

I was confident there is more in our datasets than just nice pictures, so I systematically analysed 6 years of archived datasets and verified many thousands of false detections. Only one stood out, a single star that quickly varied visibly in brightness. The detection was made with only 3 images into the session, so I was very lucky to capture it. Initially I was unsure of what I had detected, so I researched the star behaviour, but because it didn’t match the usual characteristics of a variable star, I wasn’t confident in the detection. I checked the star designation on the Variable Star Index (VSX) and found that the star 2MASS J03293430+3117433 wasn’t known to be variable.

Life then got in the way, and it took me just over a year to attend a public lecture at ASSA to ask the bright minds here what I could do with this detection. I was surprised to meet one of my former university lecturers, Andrew Wendelborn, who informed me that I might have discovered a variable star, and introduced me to David Benn.

I met David and shared my dataset for him to analyse with Tycho Tracker and VStar. He confirmed that the star was significantly variable, and that the star was not currently listed as variable in the VSX. David plotted a light curve for the star’s behavior, and determined its behavior is like that of a stellar flare rather than a more traditional variable, which has a more consistent variation in brightness over hours, days, months or years.

The star itself is near NGC 1333, which is classified by NASA as a star-forming region. SIMBAD designates this star as a Young Stellar Object, and based on my research young stars are more susceptible to flaring. This is because young stars often spin faster, causing their atmosphere to become more turbulent. This then causes magnetic fields to become tangled, break, and then reconnect, releasing significant amounts of energy and light.

Over the following few months David and Andrew both supported me in further analysing the data and making a submission to VSX. While the submission was being worked on, Andrew presented the discovery at NACAA in Tamworth over Easter 2026 (see the June 2026 Bulletin).

Overall, I found the VSX submission process unintuitive and quite challenging to understand. I learned during this process that the “Remark” field is supposed to contain a description of the star rather than a small summary of what had been observed. Another field “Revision Comment” is used to capture references rather than why the star entry was being revised.

After several revisions, and with guidance from Sebastián Otero (AAVSO) and David, the discovery was accepted by Sebastián at VSX on the 28th of August 2026. Andrew mentioned that not many stellar flares have been captured optically in near real time and suggested I write a paper on it.

Each image we capture could hold scientific value; we just need the tenacity to dig in and find what might be hiding inside. I have experienced incredible guidance and support from other ASSA members, and I’m glad I reached out to the society!
